# Fiscal impact and growth modelling

## A growth target becomes a fiscal commitment long before the money is spent

A population target, housing forecast, employment projection, infrastructure plan, or revenue estimate rarely is a single number.

Behind it may sit assumptions about household formation, land availability, development density, market absorption, infrastructure capacity, construction timing, capital cost, operating cost, tax base, borrowing, grants, service levels, and policy.

Those assumptions interact. DDA opens the headline number and reconstructs the system beneath it.

The objective is to determine what must be true for the forecast to hold, where the system may constrain it, what the fiscal consequences are under different conditions, and which uncertainties can actually change the decision.

The result is a model that can be inspected, challenged, updated, and used.

## When the numbers need to be opened

The question may arrive as a population target, housing requirement, land-demand estimate, infrastructure request, capital plan, revenue forecast, development scenario, or long-range financial projection.

The number may already appear in an official plan, budget, staff report, consultant study, business case, or policy commitment.

That does not mean the analytical chain beneath it has been established.

A population projection may use a different geography from the servicing plan. A housing target may assume household formation rates that do not match the population scenario. A land calculation may count theoretical acreage without accounting for servicing, ownership, environmental constraints, fragmentation, or market availability. A revenue estimate may recognize assessment growth without carrying the corresponding capital and operating liabilities.

Even apparently simple comparisons can fail when the underlying definitions, denominators, dates, or units are different.

DDA begins by reconstructing the chain.

**What is the number? Where did it come from? What does it actually measure? What assumptions connect it to the decision?**

Every material input is classified before it is relied upon. Observed evidence is kept separate from derived values, proxies, modelled assumptions, and unresolved gaps.

That creates an evidence base before the model begins producing answers.

## From headline target to delivery system

Growth is not one variable moving upward.

It is a chain of connected stocks, flows, capacities, decisions, and delays.

A population scenario can change the number of households. Household formation can change housing demand. Housing demand can change land requirements. Land requirements can create servicing requirements. Servicing creates capital expenditure, operating expenditure, maintenance liabilities, debt requirements, and timing dependencies.

At the same time, growth may expand assessment, user-fee, development-charge, utility, or other revenues.

The fiscal question includes two parts:

**How much revenue does growth create?**

**What must be built, expanded, maintained, staffed, financed, and eventually replaced to support it?**

DDA connects those relationships instead of modelling them as isolated lines.

A typical analytical chain may run:

**Population to households to housing demand to development capacity to serviced land to infrastructure demand to capital program to operating requirements to revenue to net fiscal position.**

For employment or industrial growth, the chain may instead connect:

**Employment or production to land and facility requirements to infrastructure and workforce constraints to investment timing to tax or royalty base to public costs to fiscal consequence.**

The exact architecture depends on the decision.

The principle remains the same: **every material number remains connected to the condition that produces it.**

## Capacity on paper and capacity that can be delivered

One of the most important distinctions in growth modelling is the difference between **theoretical capacity and deliverable capacity**.

They are not interchangeable.

A parcel may permit development under policy and still be unavailable to the market. Land may exist but lack servicing. Infrastructure may have nominal capacity but depend on an upstream project. A housing allocation may fit mathematically while exceeding realistic construction or absorption rates. A capital project may appear in a ten-year plan without being designed, permitted, funded, procured, or deliverable on the assumed schedule.

DDA therefore tests capacity through successive filters.

**Theoretical capacity:** What could exist under the stated density, land-use, demographic, or policy assumptions?

**Physical capacity:** What can the land and infrastructure actually support?

**Serviceable capacity:** What can be connected to the required transportation, water, wastewater, energy, or other systems?

**Market capacity:** What can plausibly be absorbed or constructed within the relevant period?

**Institutional capacity:** What can approvals, procurement, staffing, financing, and project delivery systems process?

**Policy-adjusted capacity:** What remains once the applicable policy choices, exclusions, priorities, and service standards are applied?

**Time-adjusted capacity:** What can actually become available when the decision requires it?

This distinction can materially change both growth and fiscal conclusions.

A community may have enough land for twenty years of theoretical demand while having only five years of realistically serviceable supply.

A municipality may have enough infrastructure capacity in aggregate while a single treatment plant, intersection, pipe, funding decision, or approval becomes the binding constraint.

A housing target may be physically possible but impossible on the assumed timetable.

The useful number is **capacity under the conditions that govern delivery**.

## The DDA modelling framework

DDA approaches fiscal and growth modelling as a decision system, not a spreadsheet exercise.

Several analytical tests operate together.

### Evidence architecture

Every material input is traced to its source, date, definition, geography, unit, and role in the model.

Published evidence, client data, derived calculations, proxies, assumptions, and unresolved gaps are kept distinct.

This prevents a modelled assumption from quietly acquiring the authority of an observed fact.

### System structure

The model identifies the stocks, flows, dependencies, feedback loops, delays, thresholds, and bottlenecks that connect growth to fiscal consequence.

This matters because systems do not always respond proportionally.

A small delay in servicing can postpone development. Deferred infrastructure can cross a cost threshold. New infrastructure can create recurring operating and replacement liabilities. Additional capacity can induce demand elsewhere in the system.

The model looks for these relationships instead of assuming a straight line.

### Constraint sequencing

Not every constraint matters at the same time.

Land may appear to be the limiting factor until servicing is examined. Servicing may appear binding until project-delivery capacity is tested. Financing may appear decisive until market absorption is introduced.

DDA asks:

**Which condition binds first?**

Then:

**What becomes binding if that condition is resolved?**

This exposes the sequence between a target and delivery.

### Competing explanations

A fiscal gap or capacity shortfall can have more than one cause.

For example, a projected infrastructure deficit could reflect genuine resource insufficiency. It could also reflect timing, project sequencing, an overstated service requirement, a weak land assumption, premature infrastructure expansion, cost escalation, delivery friction, or some combination of these.

DDA tests plausible explanations against the evidence instead of selecting the first coherent story.

### Decision discipline

Models can inherit the assumptions of the people building them.

DDA therefore tests for anchoring to existing targets, excessive reliance on recent experience, false precision, optimistic timelines, inconsistent judgments, hidden framing choices, and the tendency to replace a difficult question with an easier one.

Where judgment is required, the components are made explicit before the overall conclusion is formed.

### Representation test

Administrative categories make complex systems manageable. They can also conceal important variation.

A single category such as “developable land,” “housing capacity,” “infrastructure need,” “population,” or “capital cost” may combine materially different things.

DDA asks what has been aggregated, what disappears inside the category, and whether the model needs to be taken down to the parcel, infrastructure asset, housing type, taxpayer class, project, month, or other decision-relevant unit.

The map is tested against the territory.

### Actor and incentive test

Growth and fiscal outcomes are produced by institutions and people, not equations alone.

Developers, municipalities, utilities, senior governments, landowners, regulators, lenders, residents, infrastructure operators, and other actors may control different parts of the delivery chain.

DDA examines what each actor controls, what constrains them, what they have an incentive to do, what they must preserve, and how they may respond when conditions change.

A scenario that requires an actor to behave against its incentives without a mechanism for changing those incentives is not yet a credible scenario.

### Friction test

Plans operate under real conditions.

Approvals take time. Projects encounter procurement constraints. Construction markets tighten. Costs move. Staff capacity is finite. Information arrives late. Funding programs change. Projects interact. Small failures accumulate.

DDA tests the model against these conditions instead of assuming frictionless execution.

## What gets tested

The analysis is built around the variables capable of changing the result.

Depending on the question, DDA may test:

- population and demographic assumptions;
- household formation;
- housing demand and housing mix;
- employment and economic growth;
- land supply and parcel capacity;
- density and development yield;
- servicing availability and infrastructure thresholds;
- development timing;
- market absorption;
- capital cost;
- operating and maintenance cost;
- inflation and escalation;
- debt and financing assumptions;
- assessment and revenue growth;
- user-fee or rate impacts;
- grants and external funding;
- policy changes;
- project-delivery capacity;
- regulatory or approval dependencies;
- service standards;
- lifecycle and replacement liabilities;
- intergovernmental or cross-jurisdictional dependencies.

The purpose is to find the variables that carry the decision, without maximizing the number of variables.

A model becomes more useful when it can say:

**This assumption matters. These five do not materially change the choice. If this threshold is crossed, the conclusion changes.**

That is different from simply making the model larger.

## Scenarios, uncertainty, and thresholds

A scenario is a statement about consequences under defined conditions. It is not a prediction.

DDA normally establishes a defensible baseline and then tests credible alternatives around the variables that matter most.

The alternatives may include slower or faster growth, different development patterns, infrastructure delays, higher capital costs, different absorption rates, altered financing conditions, policy changes, or combinations of these factors.

The analysis then asks three questions.

**How far does the result move?**

**Why does it move?**

**At what point does the decision change?**

That third question is often the most valuable.

A municipality may not need to know whether the “correct” growth rate is 1.7 or 1.9 per cent. It may need to know whether infrastructure expansion becomes necessary above 2.3 per cent.

A project may remain fiscally positive across a wide range of construction costs but become unattractive if delivery slips three years.

A land strategy may work across several density assumptions but fail if a particular servicing project is delayed.

These are **decision thresholds**.

DDA makes them visible.

Uncertainty is kept out of a single forecast and made part of the decision architecture.

## Fiscal consequence over time

Growth can improve a fiscal position while still creating periods of significant financial pressure.

Timing matters.

Revenue and cost do not necessarily arrive together.

Infrastructure may have to be built before assessment growth appears. Debt may be incurred before development charges or user revenues are collected. New assets may create immediate operating costs and long-term renewal obligations. A development pattern that produces similar revenue to another may require substantially more infrastructure per household.

DDA therefore looks beyond the gross revenue effect.

Where the evidence permits, the model separates:

**Capital consequence:** What must be built, expanded, acquired, or replaced?

**Operating consequence:** What recurring staffing, maintenance, utility, service, and administrative costs follow?

**Revenue consequence:** What taxes, rates, fees, charges, transfers, royalties, or other revenues change?

**Timing consequence:** When do costs occur relative to revenue?

**Financing consequence:** What must be borrowed, reserved, funded externally, or carried?

**Lifecycle consequence:** What future maintenance and replacement obligation is created by today's growth decision?

**Net fiscal consequence:** What remains once the relevant costs and revenues are connected through time?

This prevents revenue growth from being mistaken for fiscal improvement.

## What the work may produce

The output is designed around the decision and the people who must use it.

A DDA engagement may produce:

**Growth model:** Population, household, employment, housing, land, or economic-growth scenarios connected through explicit assumptions.

**Capacity reconciliation:** A structured comparison of theoretical, physical, serviceable, market, institutional, policy-adjusted, and time-adjusted capacity.

**Parcel or land model:** Development capacity taken to the spatial unit required to test the headline assumption.

**Infrastructure dependency register:** The projects, assets, approvals, funding decisions, and external dependencies that determine when growth can occur.

**Fiscal model:** Capital, operating, revenue, financing, and lifecycle consequences connected through time.

**Scenario matrix:** A comparison of credible alternative futures and the conditions producing each result.

**Sensitivity and threshold analysis:** The assumptions capable of changing the decision and the point at which they do.

**Evidence register:** The source, date, definition, geography, status, and limitations of material inputs.

**Assumptions register:** A visible record of what has been assumed, why it was necessary, and where it enters the model.

**Contradiction log:** Material conflicts between sources, definitions, periods, figures, or interpretations that must be resolved or carried as uncertainty.

**Decision brief:** A concise account of what the evidence establishes, what remains conditional, where the principal constraints sit, and what would change the conclusion.

**Technical appendix:** The calculations and source trail required for another analyst, reviewer, council, board, ministry, or advisor to retrace the result.

The model is intended to be transparent. The reader should be able to see how the answer was produced.

## Decision instrument

### Scenario architecture

**01: Baseline evidence:** What is directly established, from which source, for which geography and period?

**02: Growth assumptions:** What changes under the scenario, and what remains constant?

**03: Capacity and constraints:** Which land, infrastructure, market, institutional, financial, or policy condition limits delivery first?

**04: Dependencies and actor response:** What has to happen, who controls it, and what could delay or change it?

**05: Fiscal consequence:** What happens to capital requirements, operating costs, revenues, financing, and lifecycle obligations?

**06: Feedback and timing:** What consequences return to affect later growth, capacity, cost, or decision-making?

**07: Sensitivity finding:** Which assumption carries the result, and where is the decision threshold?

**08: Uncertainty:** What is known, what is modelled, what remains unresolved, and what evidence would change the conclusion?

Every input remains connected to the decision it can change.

### A model should survive the hardest question in the room

Growth and fiscal models often move into important settings.

They may inform a council decision, capital program, development negotiation, infrastructure request, policy change, investment decision, public commitment, or long-range plan.

The analysis should therefore remain usable when someone challenges the source, denominator, assumption, geography, timeframe, or causal link.

DDA builds for that challenge from the beginning.

Material claims trace back to their evidence. Assumptions are exposed. Alternative explanations are tested. Uncertainty is stated without being hidden behind precision. Where the evidence is silent, the model says so.

The purpose is to make clear **which uncertainty matters to the decision**.
