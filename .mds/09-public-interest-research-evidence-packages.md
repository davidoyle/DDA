# Institutional and policy analysis

Public claims and institutional questions often cross mandates, budgets, decisions, communications, appeals, enforcement, and implementation. DDA reconstructs that record so a team can see what the evidence supports, what conflicts, and what is still unresolved. Then DDA explains why the institution produced the outcome it did.

## When the record is fragmented

The work can start with a public statement, a policy proposal, an institutional response, an oversight question, or a set of records that nobody has assembled. Repetition does not make a claim well supported. Several reports can trace back to one source, use different definitions, or leave the critical implementation step undocumented.

DDA builds the chronology, follows each claim to its primary source, and maps the actors and decision points. When sources use different definitions, reporting periods, or categories, DDA makes them comparable before any comparison occurs.

The record also shows what is missing. A mandate with no budget line, a warning signal with no documented response, or a decision with no recorded reason is a finding. DDA records each absence with the same care as the evidence that exists.

## Test what the evidence can support

DDA defines the test before it reads the record. If a minister commits to stronger enforcement, the analysis first states what delivery of that commitment means and how to measure it. This keeps the analysis away from a search for evidence that confirms a view.

A claim-and-source register records what each claim says, the source and date behind it, the evidence that conflicts with it, and the inference the material can responsibly carry. Every entry has a status. ACTUAL means a primary source directly supports it. PROXY means the evidence measures something close to the claim, and the register states the difference. FLAG means the conclusion depends on a condition that no evidence yet establishes.

The register also compares what an institution said with what it did in the same period. When an institution denies a problem and funds an urgent response to it in the same month, both facts are evidence.

DDA tests competing explanations against observable evidence. Material gaps stay visible, with what depends on them and where resolution is possible. The approach supports policy, legal, media, advocacy, oversight, and public-sector teams, and it treats an allegation as an allegation until the evidence says otherwise.

## Explain how the institution decided

A verified record shows what occurred. The next question is why the institution produced that outcome, and it needs a different set of tests.

**Rules on paper and rules in use.** The formal rule and the working rule often differ. DDA separates three levels: the rules that govern daily operations, the rules that control how those rules change, and the rules that decide who can make that change. An institution works by the rules its people know, expect others to monitor, and expect someone to enforce. DDA asks what rule formally applies, what rule people actually follow, who can change each one, and what occurs when someone breaks it.

**Decision architecture.** An actor map shows who took part. DDA reconstructs how the decision was assembled. Different parts of an organization supply the factual and value premises of a decision, and authority, information, advice, loyalty, and communication paths all shape the result. DDA asks who supplied each premise, who had authority to decide, and who had information without authority. It also asks where someone filtered, withheld, or ignored information.

**Incentives, dependencies, and power.** An institution can know that a policy underperforms and still have no reason to correct it. Actors protect budgets, jurisdiction, coalitions, discretion, reputation, and survival. Formal public objectives do not always explain how an institution spends money or how it behaves. For each important actor, DDA asks what it wants, what it must protect, what it controls, what it depends on, and what will change its behaviour.

**Strategic interaction and credible commitment.** Many policies ask a different actor to change its behaviour: a lower level of government, a regulated firm, a funding recipient, or a contractor. Those actors anticipate each other. Promises, sanctions, signals, and commitments that bind future choices often decide the outcome. DDA asks why the other actor will comply, and what makes the desired behaviour credible. This test matters most for intergovernmental policy, regulation, procurement, funding agreements, negotiations, and voluntary compliance.

## Follow the system over time

Implementation runs over time. A working institution needs feedback, a way to detect when results move away from intent, and a mechanism that responds. DDA asks what information comes back from implementation, to whom, after what delay, and if the institution acts on it.

Delay distorts judgment in two directions. A sound decision can look ineffective before its effects arrive, and a failure can compound for years before anyone reacts. DDA measures the gap between the date a signal was available and the date the institution responded. That lag is a result, and the analysis reports it as one.

People who experience a decline can leave or complain, and the institution can ignore both. A complaints process can exist and correct nothing, so DDA tests if complaints change decisions.

DDA also measures conversion: how much activity becomes outcome. The ratio of enforcement actions to charges, or of announced priorities to funded capacity, shows quickly where a mechanism fails.

A sound mechanism can still fail in execution. Capacity, sequence, handoffs, unclear authority, poor communication, human error, and the response of other actors all create friction, and small failures accumulate. DDA treats friction as an object of analysis with its own evidence.

## Examine what the institution cannot see

Administrative systems make reality legible by simplifying it into categories, metrics, eligibility rules, maps, and reporting fields. Each simplification removes some variation, local knowledge, and informal practice. Evidence can be correct inside the administrative model while the model misrepresents conditions on the ground. DDA asks what the institution has reduced to a category, what that category hides, and where practice differs from the formal representation.

Causes also sit at different levels: an individual, an organization, an institution, a jurisdiction, or the wider system. A common error is to blame an official for an outcome that institutional rules produced, or to blame an institution for a limit that a higher jurisdiction set. A structural constraint can also look like a communication problem. DDA identifies the level where each cause sits before it assigns responsibility.

## Test the judgment, including ours

DDA tests the reasoning of the people who make the claims, and its own. Decision-makers often treat their case as unique, anchor on the first explanation, or confuse confidence with strength of evidence. Analysts do the same. DDA asks what reference class exists, what the base rate is, and if a preferred explanation has anchored the analysis.

Policy analysis often depends on what will occur next. DDA states forecasts explicitly: what it expects, by when, with what confidence, and which observable evidence will cause an update. Probabilities appear where they help. Each forecast has a horizon and indicators, and the analysis changes when the evidence does.

## Find the crux

A complete record is still only information. The strategic step is diagnosis: of everything found, what actually explains the outcome? DDA identifies the critical constraint, the one that is consequential and possible to act on, and states what changes if that constraint changes.

One distinction carries a lot of weight. Public debate usually assumes that a failing institution needs more resources. Sometimes the resources are present and the design is broken. The register can separate the two, and the answer decides if a budget increase will help.

## Forms the work can take

A scoped assignment can produce a primary-source register, a chronology, an actor or decision map, a policy-mechanism analysis, a hypothesis and falsification structure, a briefing memo, a publication-ready report, a verification appendix, a plain-language version, or a correction log.

The verification appendix gives a reproducible access path for every claim: the URL, the database query, or the document page. Any reader can check any claim without help from DDA.

## Scope and limits

This is research and analysis. It is not legal representation, privileged work, medical advice, or investment advice, and it does not find that an allegation is true. Sensitive personal, Indigenous, confidential, or commercially restricted material needs an agreed scope, authorization, and handling method.
